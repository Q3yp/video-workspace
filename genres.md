# Genres

A film takes one genre. Company films also follow "Company films", whichever genre they take.

## Company films

A company film is held to the standard of the best company films in its genre. The failure to avoid is a product demo: a story built around the product's workflow comes out narrow, and the product ends up answering a question nobody asked.

- **Research before writing.** Study the client's industry and its customers, and the best company films in the chosen genre, from any industry as well as the client's category. Collect facts with sources that the film can stand on. The story comes from this material, not from the feature list.
- **Set the stakes wider than the product.** Find what the product changes beyond its own features, and how far out the film can travel from one person or animal.
- **Frame the value through the customer's lasting goal.** Lead with what the client's customers most want from their work, and treat newer angles as consequences of that goal.
- **Put people and animals at the centre.** The product enables them.
- **Show the company at work in its customers' whole operation.** Other makers' machines and buildings appear freely, unlabelled, as they do on a real site.
- **Name a product only where it does that work.** A label sits on the shot where the product acts, and a figure or record is credited only to the system that produces it.

## Brand film

Bring the product in late, as the answer to a question the opening has raised; every scene before the reveal builds that question, and the product stays in the background until then. Restraint reads as premium: sparse narration, long holds, slow and motivated camera moves, natural light, quiet frames. Name the product once, after the reveal.

## Motion graphics

Modern and tech-feeling, with graphics carrying the film over live footage.

### Story

- The story is built from events happening now that move fast by themselves. A spoken statement can carry the film, with its key words set on screen word by word as they are said.
- The product's interface and data can be on screen from the first scene. Each product appears in the shot where it works, with its name as a label on the equipment it drives.
- The brand can open the film as well as close it: a few seconds built from the same graphic system, ending on the film's first frame.

### Graphic system

- The graphics are one system built from the client's logo geometry. Its smallest repeatable shape is the particle: in large numbers it forms fields that flow, swirl, burst and line up, and it cuts the masks that frame the footage. Its shapes, angles and colours set every panel, mask, graphic cut and transition.
- The film can end by assembling the logo from those pieces, finishing on the original logo file rather than a redrawn copy. The assembly starts from a shape already on screen and travels into the logo's own geometry; a shape that only resembles the logo reads as a different mark.
- Full-frame graphics and type on flat brand colours alternate with footage seen through the masks, and each section takes its own colour from the palette.
- Spans of time and quantities are told by graphics that count or tick past, in seconds, rather than by scenes.
- A graphic sits on what it describes, in the scene, and follows it when the camera moves; plan a locked camera or a track for it in the shot prompt.
- A product label carries a reading that changes with the scene, a small live chart or a counting figure, rather than a slogan.
- Figures on screen are illustrative readings unless the client supplies them as results.

### Pace

- Footage cuts every one to two seconds, while a graphic passage runs as one continuous move for several seconds.
- Cuts, words and graphic entrances land on the beat. Camera moves are dynamic and land on a stable frame, and transitions vary from scene to scene.
- The first seconds carry the genre at full strength: the first frame lands on a hit, the opening shots cut at the film's pace, and the graphics are there from the first second.
